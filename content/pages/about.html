About SAKSHI

A proposed platform for community auditors to investigate clinical AI after deployment.

Relationship to MIT Critical Data

SAKSHI is a project of MIT Critical Data, the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The framework was developed by a multidisciplinary team of clinicians and clinical trainees, public-health and informatics researchers, and data scientists, several of them working in the deployment regions the platform targets.

The consortium's wider institutional partnerships are disclosed publicly and managed through a firewall that keeps commercial relationships from shaping which audits are chosen, how they are run, or what they conclude. An independent editorial board drawn from outside the consortium oversees that firewall. See Governance.

Where things stand

SAKSHI is a proposal under development. The paper describes the proposed design and governance model. It has not been evaluated.

  • No profiles have been published.
  • The case study is a hypothetical composite.
  • None of the framework's comparability or feasibility claims have been tested against real profiles produced by independently constituted teams.
  • The Delphi-based process for setting shared evaluation thresholds is still under development. Its ability to reduce differences in auditor judgments across hubs has not been validated.
  • The first regional hubs are being formed. See Get Involved.

What SAKSHI cannot do

SAKSHI cannot force a company to change its practices or withdraw a product. It cannot require compensation for patients who were harmed.

SAKSHI will maintain a permanent public evidence record for journalists, health ministries and procurement officials, legal advocates, patient organizations, and regulators. These groups could use the findings to seek changes. SAKSHI could supply evidence but could not determine whether they act.

Some companies may continue unchanged after a critical profile. The record will allow readers to assess their subsequent actions.

Open empirical questions

The paper proposes the following tests once the first hubs are operating.

Does an investigation uncover evidence beyond existing disclosures?
For a small initial set of real deployments, the team will compare SAKSHI findings with regulatory filings and public disclosures available to companies or ministries of health. Organizing existing information into an accessible record could be useful. Establishing that local investigation adds evidence will require findings beyond those disclosures, such as missing local validation, undisclosed financial relationships, or undocumented consent practices.
Do differently composed local auditor teams converge on comparable judgments?
Independent auditor teams will apply the protocol to the same deployment. Agreement will be measured separately for each domain because agreement may differ across domains. This test will assess whether the shared thresholds developed through the Delphi process support comparable judgments.
Does training prepare auditors to assess evidence in practice?
The proposed certification process ends with a supervised field contribution assessed by a senior auditor. Evaluators who took no part in certification will then review newly certified auditors’ profiles using the training rubric. This will test whether performance on a reference case predicts performance in a live investigation.

These tests require profiles produced by independently constituted teams. The first hubs will generate the data needed to carry them out.

This website

This site’s source is kept in a public GitHub repository, where readers can inspect its edit history. Each profile will be generated from a structured data file. Corrections to published findings will be recorded in that history.

The site is static, carries no analytics or tracking, and uses no third-party scripts. Content is licensed CC BY 4.0.
